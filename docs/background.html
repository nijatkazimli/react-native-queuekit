Background and native tasks

A JavaScript queue only runs while JavaScript runs. This page explains what that means on a phone, and the three layers QueueKit gives you to make jobs finish anyway.

Background execution is best effort

iOS and Android decide when, and whether, background work runs, based on battery, usage patterns, Low Power Mode, Doze and vendor-specific rules. Treat everything in layers 2 and 3 as "helps in practice", never as a guarantee. Layer 1 and the native tasks in layer 4 are the dependable ones.

Layer 1: catch up when the app returns

While the app was suspended, timers did not fire and connectivity may have changed without the queue noticing. flush() re-reads connectivity and starts whatever is due.

It calls queue.flush() every time the app state becomes "active". It returns a function that stops listening.

Layer 2: periodic background tasks

Both platforms can wake your app now and then to do a little work. Inside that window, await queue.drain({ timeoutMs }) runs everything it can and returns when the queue is idle or when your time budget is used up.

Rules of thumb

  • Always pass timeoutMs. Finish a few seconds before the OS ends your window. drain returns false if time ran out.
  • Import your job modules in the task file. The task may run without your UI, so a handler that was only registered by a screen will be missing and its jobs will not run.
  • Handlers must be idempotent. A job still running when the OS kills the process runs again later.
  • drain ignores jobs that cannot run (offline, no handler, detached, due after your timeout), so it will not hold the window open for them.
  • Do not expect an exact schedule. Fifteen minutes is the minimum, not a promise.

Layer 4: native tasks with detach

For large uploads and downloads, do not run the transfer in JavaScript at all. Hand it to the OS's own transfer service, which keeps going while the app is suspended and can finish even if the app is terminated. QueueKit keeps tracking the job.

The flow

  1. The handler starts the native transfer and gets a task id.
  2. It returns ctx.detach(taskId). The job stays active with job.externalId = taskId, frees its concurrency slot and has no timeout.
  3. When the native task ends, you call queue.complete(jobId) or queue.fail(jobId, error).
  4. fail() goes through the normal retry logic: the handler runs again (a fresh upload) or the job becomes failed.
  5. After an app restart, detached jobs are still active. At startup, look them up and reattach listeners.

NativeUploader stands for whatever uploader you use (react-native-background-upload, a native module of your own, and so on). The pattern is the same: start, detach, report back.

Finished while the app was dead

If the OS completed the transfer while your app was not running, nothing tells the queue. Make sure your uploader reports pending results on next launch, or check your backend for the object by its idempotency key, then call complete() or fail(). Do this inside reattachUploads().

A sensible combination

  1. Always: flushOnForeground(queue, AppState).
  2. Small jobs (API calls, forms): rely on the queue plus a periodic background drain.
  3. Big files: detach() to a native uploader.
  4. Make every handler idempotent, and always send job.id as an idempotency key.
  5. Test with the app killed from the app switcher and with airplane mode toggled.
